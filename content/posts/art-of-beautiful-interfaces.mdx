The architecture of the post-1945 world order — the United Nations, the IMF, the World Bank, the WTO, NATO — was always a reflection of American power. Its genius lay in disguising hegemony as multilateralism. For nearly eight decades, that fiction held. Today, it is fraying in plain sight.

## The Symptoms Are Everywhere

Russia's invasion of Ukraine exposed the limits of international law as a deterrent mechanism. The UN Security Council, paralysed by the veto, watched as a permanent member launched the largest land war in Europe since 1945. The ICJ issued binding orders that were simply ignored.

In the Indo-Pacific, China's Belt and Road Initiative has effectively created a parallel infrastructure of economic dependency — one that deliberately bypasses Western-dominated institutions. By one estimate, BRI has extended financing to over 140 countries, many of which have since moderated their criticism of Beijing at the UNHRC and elsewhere.

Meanwhile, the BRICS grouping — once dismissed as an acronym in search of a strategy — expanded in 2024 to include Saudi Arabia, Iran, Ethiopia, Egypt, Argentina, and the UAE. The bloc now accounts for roughly 36% of global GDP by purchasing power parity, surpassing the G7 for the first time.

## The Three Pressures

Three structural forces are driving this fragmentation:

**1. The End of Unipolarity**

The United States remains the world's most powerful state by most measures — military spending, reserve currency dominance, technological leadership. But preponderance is not the same as hegemony. When the gap between your capabilities and everyone else's narrows, your ability to impose your preferred rules narrows with it.

China's defence budget, now the world's second largest, has grown 400% in real terms since 2000. Russia has demonstrated that even a severely sanctioned economy can sustain a protracted conflict. The hegemon's veto over acceptable international behaviour is eroding.

**2. The Weaponisation of Interdependence**

Economists once believed that trade created peace — that nations too commercially intertwined to afford conflict. The 2010s demolished this theory. The United States sanctioned Iran into economic isolation. China used economic coercion against Lithuania, Australia, and South Korea. Russia weaponised gas dependency. Europe discovered that supply chains are not neutral infrastructure — they are levers.

The consequence is *friend-shoring*: the systematic effort to disentangle strategic supply chains from geopolitical adversaries. This is happening in semiconductors, rare earths, pharmaceuticals, and energy. The integration paradigm of the 1990s is being reversed. The question is how far that reversal goes.

**3. The Legitimacy Deficit**

The rules-based international order has always had a legitimacy problem: its rules were written by the victors of 1945. The Global South's resentment of this asymmetry — long simmering — has found political expression in the Ukraine conflict, where most developing nations have refused to impose sanctions on Russia, reading the West's outrage as selective.

Narendra Modi's India, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's Brazil, and Cyril Ramaphosa's South Africa have all explicitly positioned themselves as neither Western nor Russian — a posture that would have been diplomatically costly a decade ago. It is now a source of leverage.

## What Comes After?

History does not offer reassuring precedents for hegemonic transitions. The shift from British to American dominance in the early 20th century was mediated by two world wars. The transition from a unipolar to a multipolar order — if that is indeed what is happening — has no established playbook.

Several futures are plausible. One is a **managed multipolarity**: regional blocs with their own institutions and spheres of influence, some degree of inter-bloc cooperation on global commons (climate, pandemics), but fundamentally separate political economies. This is sometimes called *bloc-isation*.

A second is **hegemonic persistence with adaptation**: the United States rebuilds its alliances, reinvests in its technological edge, and finds a way to extend its institutional architecture without demanding ideological conformity from partners. The QUAD, AUKUS, and the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework are early experiments in this direction.

A third — most dangerous — is an **interregnum**: a period of institutional collapse without replacement, where the norms governing force, trade, and sovereignty degrade simultaneously, and the risk of catastrophic conflict rises accordingly.

The decisions made in Washington, Beijing, Brussels, and New Delhi over the next decade will determine which path the world takes. The stakes are not abstract.
