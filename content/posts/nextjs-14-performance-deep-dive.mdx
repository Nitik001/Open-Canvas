The dollar's reign as the world's reserve currency is one of the most consequential facts of modern economic life. It determines who pays exchange risk, who can run current account deficits with impunity, and who can weaponise capital flows as foreign policy. The "exorbitant privilege", as Valéry Giscard d'Estaing called it in 1965, has compounded for eighty years.

Now, for perhaps the first time, there is a plausible structural argument — not just rhetorical desire — for why that privilege might diminish. Whether it will is a different question.

## The Demand-Side Case

The case for dollar primacy has rested on three pillars: network effects (everyone uses it because everyone else uses it), deep capital markets (no other market offers US Treasury depth and liquidity), and institutional trust (the Federal Reserve, whatever its flaws, is credible).

All three remain largely intact. The dollar's share of global foreign exchange reserves has fallen from 71% in 1999 to roughly 58% today — a decline that sounds significant until you note that no single alternative has absorbed it. The euro accounts for 20%, and the renminbi — despite years of internationalisation efforts — barely exceeds 3%.

That renminbi figure is the crux of the de-dollarisation debate. China has unambiguously sought to raise the yuan's international profile: bilateral currency swap lines with 40+ central banks, the Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS), yuan-denominated commodity contracts in Shanghai, and the digital yuan as an alternative payment rail.

Yet the constraints are structural. Internationalisation requires capital account openness — the ability of foreigners to freely move money in and out. China maintains a closed capital account, for good reason: an open one would expose its financial system to the kind of speculative flows that devastated Southeast Asia in 1997. The currency cannot be truly international while the capital account is controlled. Beijing knows this. It is choosing stability over reach.

## The Supply-Side Shock: Sanctions as Accelerant

Where de-dollarisation *has* accelerated is on the supply side — driven not by Chinese ambition but by American policy choices.

The freezing of Russia's $300 billion in central bank reserves after the 2022 invasion was a watershed moment. It demonstrated, for the first time, that dollar-denominated reserves held in Western jurisdictions were not safe stores of value for governments that fell out of favour in Washington. The sanction was effective — but it sent a signal to every emerging market government: your reserves are only yours so long as your politics please the hegemon.

The response has been rational portfolio diversification. Central banks have dramatically increased gold purchases — a bearer asset that cannot be frozen. India settled crude imports from Russia in rupees. Saudi Arabia began accepting yuan for Chinese oil purchases. These are not ideological statements; they are risk management decisions.

## The Structural Limits of the Challenge

Against this backdrop, three structural features will constrain de-dollarisation for the foreseeable future:

**1. There is no substitute.** The yuan is controlled. The euro lacks a common fiscal backstop and faces persistent fragmentation risk. No other currency offers the combination of depth, liquidity, and institutional trust that the dollar provides.

**2. Trade invoicing inertia.** Roughly 88% of global currency transactions and over 50% of trade invoices involve the dollar. Switching is costly. Contracts, derivatives, and commodity markets are built on dollar rails. The SWIFT alternative (CIPS) handles a fraction of global flows and remains technically inferior.

**3. The US financial system is a feature, not a bug.** Even adversaries hold dollar assets because US capital markets are uniquely efficient at allocating capital. China holds $800 billion in US Treasuries not out of goodwill, but because there is no better risk-adjusted alternative at that scale.

## A Fragmented, Not De-dollarised, World

The most probable outcome is not de-dollarisation but **currency fragmentation**: a world of parallel financial systems, where the dollar dominates Western-aligned trade and finance, the yuan becomes the reference currency in parts of Asia and Africa, and bilateral currency arrangements fill specific niches.

This is already happening. It is economically less efficient than a single reserve currency system — an additional friction tax on global commerce. But it is resilient to political shocks in a way that the old system was not.

The dollar will not be toppled. But its authority will be diluted — gradually, unevenly, and largely as a consequence of the choices made in Washington, not Beijing.
