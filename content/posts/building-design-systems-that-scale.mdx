For thirty years, the consensus view among Western economists and policymakers was clear: governments are bad at picking winners, markets allocate capital more efficiently than states, and industrial policy was a polite name for politically-motivated subsidy. The structural adjustment programmes of the 1980s and 1990s were built on this premise. So was the WTO rulebook.

That consensus is now over. From Washington to Brussels to Tokyo, governments are once again deploying state resources to shape the structure of their economies — explicitly targeting strategic industries, domestic supply chains, and the green energy transition. The question is not whether industrial policy has returned. It has. The question is whether it will work.

## The Triggering Events

Two developments catalysed the policy reversal with unusual speed.

**The semiconductor shock.** The 2021 global chip shortage — which halted automobile production, delayed consumer electronics, and exposed the concentration of advanced chip manufacturing in Taiwan — demonstrated in visceral terms the cost of offshoring strategic production. A supply chain disruption triggered by a pandemic had cascaded into a national security vulnerability. The CHIPS and Science Act (2022), which allocated $52 billion for domestic semiconductor manufacturing and R&D, was the bipartisan response.

**The climate crisis.** The Inflation Reduction Act (2022), despite its name, was primarily a $369 billion industrial policy instrument. It offered production tax credits for clean energy, manufacturing incentives for EV batteries and components, and domestic content requirements deliberately structured to disadvantage foreign competitors — particularly Chinese clean-tech producers. The EU's response, the Net-Zero Industry Act and the European Green Deal, followed similar logic: climate policy as industrial strategy.

## What Has Changed Since the 1970s?

Previous waves of Western industrial policy — the strategic industries programmes of the 1970s, Japan's MITI-led development, South Korea's chaebol model — are remembered selectively. The failures (British Leyland, France's Plan Calcul, countless state champions that consumed subsidy without becoming competitive) are as instructive as the successes (DARPA, the Internet, GPS, Airbus).

Several things are structurally different this time:

**The competitive context.** Previous industrial policy debates assumed that market failures justified state intervention. Today's rationale adds a geopolitical dimension: even efficient markets may produce outcomes that are strategically unacceptable if they concentrate critical production in an adversary's territory. This is a different, and harder to rebut, justification.

**The tools have evolved.** Modern industrial policy relies more heavily on tax credits, procurement requirements, and R&D co-investment than on direct state ownership. These tools are less distorting and more durable — they work with market incentives rather than against them.

**The learning has been absorbed.** South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and now China have demonstrated that state-directed investment in strategic sectors can, under the right conditions, produce globally competitive industries. The East Asian developmental state is no longer a curiosity; it is a model being studied seriously in Washington and Berlin.

## The Implementation Gaps

The theory is cleaner than the practice. Three persistent problems bedevil industrial policy implementation:

**Picking winners.** Bureaucrats with good information and clear mandates can identify sectors with market failures. They are much worse at identifying specific technologies or firms that will succeed within those sectors. The history of state investment in particular technologies — the US Department of Energy's Solyndra loan, the EU's backing of specific battery chemistries — is littered with expensive mistakes.

**The subsidy spiral.** When the United States offers $52 billion for semiconductor manufacturing, Europe, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan all respond with their own packages. The result is a global subsidy competition that benefits chipmakers and TSMC shareholders rather than any particular national strategic interest. The race to the top in incentives can end up neutralising the advantage each participant sought.

**Domestic politics.** Industrial policy, once authorised, becomes a political instrument. The domestic content requirements in the IRA were driven as much by the political imperative of winning back industrial Midwest voters as by any strategic logic. Congressional earmarking, regulatory capture, and lobbying can redirect well-designed programmes toward narrow interests faster than any bureaucrat can resist.

## The Verdict Is Not Yet In

It is too early to judge the current wave of industrial policy definitively. The CHIPS Act facilities will take years to come online. The IRA's clean-energy investment is just beginning to materialise. The EU's programmes are still being implemented.

What can be said is this: the ideological case against industrial policy has collapsed. The empirical debate — about which interventions, in which sectors, under which governance conditions, with which accountability mechanisms — is now the real argument. That is a more honest and more productive debate than the one that preceded it.

State capacity, institutional design, and political insulation from capture will determine whether this wave of industrial policy succeeds where previous waves fell short. Those are hard problems. They are not, however, reasons for policy paralysis.

The market fundamentalist moment is over. What replaces it will be shaped by experience, iteration, and — inevitably — expensive failures. The only question is whether policymakers will learn from them.
